Oral visual screening and deaths from oral cancer: results of the Kerala cluster-randomised trial

The Kerala trial compared the oral cancer mortality rate between clusters offered oral visual screening and clusters without screening. Across all eligible participants, the difference was not statistically significant (rate ratio 0.79, 95% CI 0.51 to 1.22). Among people who used tobacco, alcohol or both, the rate was lower in the screened clusters (rate ratio 0.66, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.95).

Design: 13 clusters, three screening rounds, 1996 to 2004

Investigators from the International Agency for Research on Cancer and the Regional Cancer Centre in Trivandrum randomised 13 clusters in Trivandrum district, Kerala, India. Seven clusters formed the intervention group (96,517 eligible people) and six the control group (95,356). Eligible people were aged 35 or older and had no history of oral cancer.

Cluster randomisation assigns whole areas (clusters), not individual people, to the intervention or the control group by chance. Everyone eligible in a cluster belongs to that cluster's group.

In the intervention clusters, trained health workers offered oral visual inspection in three rounds at intervals of three years. 87,655 people (91 percent) were screened at least once and 29,102 (30 percent) three times. The control clusters received no screening rounds.

The analysis compared the clusters as randomised. This is called intention to treat: every eligible person counts in the group of their cluster, whether or not they were screened. The comparison therefore measures the effect of offering screening to a population.

Results: oral cancer mortality in the intervention and control groups

The trial reports the oral cancer mortality rate in each group as deaths per 100,000 person-years. Person-years add up the time each participant was followed, so the rate takes account of different follow-up times.

The rate ratio divides the rate in the intervention group by the rate in the control group. A rate ratio of 1 means equal rates; a rate ratio below 1 means a lower rate in the intervention group. The 95% confidence interval (CI) gives the range of rate ratios compatible with the data. If the interval includes 1, the difference is not statistically significant.

In the whole study population the mortality rate was 16.4 per 100,000 person-years in the intervention group (77 deaths) and 20.7 in the control group (87 deaths). The difference was not statistically significant.

Among people who used tobacco, alcohol or both, the rates were 29.9 (70 deaths) and 45.4 (85 deaths), a rate ratio of 0.66 (0.45 to 0.95). In male users the rate ratio was 0.57 (0.35 to 0.93). In female users it was 0.78 (0.43 to 1.42), an interval that includes 1.

Attendance at referral and diagnoses among screen-positive participants

5,145 people screened positive and 3,218 (63 percent) attended referral. The other 37 percent of screen-positive people did not attend.

Among those who attended, 2,252 had oral precancerous lesions, 131 had invasive oral cancer and 835 had benign lesions or normal findings.

Oral precancerous lesions is the term the trial used. The current term is oral potentially malignant disorders: changes of the lining of the mouth that carry an increased risk of developing into cancer.

Where the result applies

The trial authors concluded that oral visual screening can reduce oral cancer mortality in people who use tobacco or alcohol.

Among people who used neither, there were 7 oral cancer deaths in the intervention group and 2 in the control group (rate ratio 3.47, 95% CI 0.12 to 96.51). With this few deaths the interval is very wide, so this subgroup gives no usable estimate of the effect of screening in people who used neither.

The trial took place in one district where tobacco chewing is common; populations with lower incidence were not studied. The article on case numbers gives incidence rates by country.

A Cochrane review (2013) included this trial as the only randomised trial of oral cancer screening and rated it at high risk of bias. Risk of bias is the reviewers' judgement of how likely the design or conduct of a trial is to distort its result.
